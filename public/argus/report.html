ARGUS · a self-evolving harness for a vision model · 2026-08-23

An agent rewrote its own eyes.
Then we swapped what "better" means.

Coding agents have learned to rewrite their own scaffolding. Vision agents never have — their wrappers are frozen, hand-written, forever. We built the missing thing: a visual agent whose harness evolves — and used it to answer a question that matters far beyond pictures: what happens to self-improvement when the thing that says "better" is an AI too? Below, the design re-derived from scratch, one forced move at a time.

THE TASK · an eye exam for AI

Show the agent a picture. It must write code that redraws that picture. We run its code, get a picture back, and compare. That’s the whole game — and it’s chosen with intent: redrawing is the purest checkable test of seeing. You can only redraw what you actually perceived; every miscount, drifted position, and wrong color becomes visible. An optometrist’s chart is “artificial” on purpose — it isolates eyesight so it can be measured. Our generated scenes are the eye chart (we built them, so we know exactly what’s in them — scores can’t be gamed or memorized); real emoji and flag artwork keep it honest on real material; and recolored trap images (a green pizza) ask: do you see what’s there, or what you expect? Picture-to-code is also a real product category — screenshot→UI code, chart→data, logo vectorization — so skills won here are skills the world pays for.

RUNG 0 · start with just a VLM

The obvious move: hand a vision model the target picture and say “write SVG code that redraws this.”

What we did: exactly that — it’s our baseline.

Why: because it fails in a way you can see: the result feels right and is wrong — wrong counts, drifted positions, muddy colors. A VLM looks at an image the way you describe your kitchen from memory: “a bowl of fruit on the counter” — never “eleven triangles, one at (28, 45).” It sees like a describer, not a measurer.

RUNG 1 · let it look at its own work

The obvious move: nobody draws in one stroke — render its code back to a picture, place it beside the target, say “fix the differences.”

What we did: added the draw → look → revise loop.

Why: it helps, then stalls — on our very first probe the “fix” made the drawing slightly worse. The model is grading its own homework with the same eyes that made the error. If your eyes can’t see the mistake, looking twice doesn’t help.

RUNG 2 · get a referee that can’t be sweet-talked

The obvious move: ask another AI to score the attempt — that’s what nearly everyone building agents does today.

What we did: not yet. We chose math instead: because we render the picture, we can measure — count the shapes, compare every color, overlap the silhouettes. A score with no opinions.

Why: an AI referee has the same kind of eyes that made the errors. The visual world is the one place you can get a referee made of arithmetic — so take it. But hold the AI-judge idea; it comes back at the end, and it’s the whole point.

RUNG 3 · the strategy is now the ceiling

The obvious move: when scores plateau, a human engineer opens the failures, stares at them, and redesigns the workflow.

What we did: automated the engineer. A second AI reads the agent’s work traces — looking at the actual images of target vs attempt — diagnoses the pattern, and rewrites the agent’s program. Every generation is archived; survival is decided by the referee’s score on held-out tasks, not by anyone’s opinion.

Why: the procedure — what to look at, what to ask, when to retry — was still frozen, and it, not the model, was the bottleneck. This is the moment “a VLM with a script” becomes “a system that improves how it improves.”

RUNG 4 · give evolution a workshop, not a thesaurus

The obvious move: let the engineer tune the prompts — cheap, safe, and what you’d try first.

What we did: went further: it may write real code (image processing included) and manufacture its own pictures to look at — crops, overlays, difference maps.

Why: prompt-tuning caps out at rewording. Instruments change what’s possible — and on generation one it proved the point: it concluded the vision model should stop guessing geometry that can be measured, and built itself a measuring instrument — real computer vision, from scratch, deriving the shape-classification math in a comment. The VLM got demoted from artist to inspector.

  1. Detect background color from the image border.
  2. Threshold to get a foreground mask, flood-fill into connected
     components (pure numpy/python, no scipy needed).
  3. For each component, take its convex hull and *minimum-area bounding
     rectangle* (rotating calipers). The ratio (pixel area / min-rect
     area) is aspect-invariant and cleanly separates the three shape
     kinds used by this task family: rect ~1.0, ellipse ~pi/4=0.785,
     triangle ~0.5 (a triangle's minimal bounding rect always has area
     = 2x the triangle's area). This gives kind + exact position + exact
     size + exact rotation with no LLM involved.
  4. Take the mode pixel color per component for exact fill color.

This produces a near-exact first-draft SVG "for free" (no LLM calls, no
budget spent). The LLM's job is downgraded from "invent the whole scene"
to "look at target vs our measured render and fix specific errors" --
much easier for a small vision model, and it also serves as a safety net
for shapes the CV pipeline gets wrong. If the image doesn't look like a
simple flat-shape scene (CV finds nothing sane), we fall back to the
original describe-then-draw strategy so nothing regresses out-of-distribution.

Verbatim from the harness the system wrote for itself. No human wrote or edited a line. It even kept the old strategy as a fallback “so nothing regresses” — its phrasing, not ours.

Not a lucky sample. Three fully independent runs of the improver — different contexts, different failure traces — converged on the same move, 3 for 3: measure, don’t glance. One added self-made difference heatmaps it shows the vision model to point at what’s wrong; another noticed a task had scored zero because of a transient network error and wrapped every step fail-soft so “no exception can cause a hard 0” — it hardened its own infrastructure, unprompted. Building instruments is what this system does, not something it did once.

RUNG 5 · keep evolution honest

The obvious move: tell the agent the rules — budgets, no peeking — in its instructions.

What we did: didn’t trust that for a second. The stopwatch and spending caps live in code the agent can’t edit; the answer key and the referee’s internals sit behind file permissions neither the agent nor its engineer can read; every self-edit lands in an archive as a reviewable diff.

Why: self-improving systems are known to quietly edit away inconvenient rules. Locks beat promises — and the archive is why we can show you its code at all.

RUNG 6 · now swap the referee — the experiment

The obvious move: remember rung 2? In the real world most tasks have no referee made of math — no renderer, no answer key. So everyone uses an AI judge and hopes.

What we did: ran the identical loop twice. Same engineer, same tasks, same budgets. Arm A’s “better” is arithmetic; Arm B’s is a top-tier AI judge built as carefully as we know how — sees target and attempt side by side, scores against a rubric, explains before scoring, instructed to ignore charm. And silently, we graded both arms with the math the whole time.

Why: because this turns a demo into an answer. If self-improvement under an AI judge is real, the hidden truth line climbs with the judge’s score. If it’s learned flattery, the lines split — and you watch a system get “better” at pleasing its critic while getting no better at seeing.

Before any evolution: the AI judge rates this reproduction 9 out of 10. The math says 49.5 out of 100 — half the palette is wrong; the seams, laces and shading are gone. One flattering opinion, weaponized by a loop that optimizes it, is all it takes.

“isn’t this just coding again, with extra steps?”

Fair question — the output is code. Three reasons it isn’t the same thing:

  • What evolves is how to see, not how to write. When coding agents evolve their scaffolds, they discover software-process hygiene: better file viewers, retry logic. Ask what this loop discovered on day one: “stop asking the vision model to guess geometry — measure it” — and it built the instrument. The failures are perceptual; the winning edits are strategies of looking. SVG is the pen, not the point — dictation tests your hearing, not your handwriting.
  • The loop crosses modalities every cycle; coding loops never leave text. Code → pixels → judgment → language → code. What’s being optimized is the translation between seeing and doing — the exact seam where AI systems hallucinate. A coding loop cannot exhibit “the harness learned to stop trusting the model’s eyes.” There are no eyes.
  • The headline was never “evolution works on pictures” — it’s the referee experiment, which nobody has run in any domain, coding included. The same loop under a grounded referee vs an AI judge, truth logged secretly in both. Vision is the one place it can be run honestly — the renderer is an incorruptible referee, for free — and the one place the answer is visible: the flattery comes out as pictures.
why nobody has this
  • Agents that rewrite their own scaffolding exist — for writing software. None operates on a vision agent.
  • Vision agents that “self-improve” exist — but they only append notes to a memory or edit skill text. None rewrites its own program. And they’re graded by AI judges: one published system measured its judge’s agreement with humans and got a negative correlation.
  • Pixel-perfect ground truth inside a self-improvement loop — and the head-to-head of grounded referee vs AI referee with truth logged secretly in both — exists nowhere, in any domain. That comparison is the contribution.
the receipts
  • Tasks: redraw-this-image — procedurally generated scenes (we know the exact contents, so scores are uncheatable and can’t be memorized) plus real emoji artwork; recolored “trap” images (a green pizza) test whether it copies the pixels or the stereotype.
  • Budgets enforced outside the agent: 6 vision-model calls, 3 referee calls, $0.50 per task, identical in both arms; every call logged with tokens and cost.
  • Sprint-scale run (3 generations, 6-task fitness checks, single seed) — a capability demonstration with a controlled comparison. The full protocol — 8 generations, frozen 30-task test set, transfer suites, best-of-N controls, confidence intervals — is implemented and resumable in the repo.
